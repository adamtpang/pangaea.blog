# Writers studied, for essay craft and video adaptation

Reference notes on real writers' technique, read directly from their actual
work (not from memory), to raise the ceiling on Pangaea essays before they
become video essays. Paraphrased, not reproduced at length; a writer's full
essays live at their own site, not copied here.

## Derek Sivers (sive.rs) — studied 2026-08

Read in full: "You have enough." (sive.rs/enough), "Prepare your 'no' and
keep it handy" (sive.rs/n0), "Obvious to you. Amazing to others."
(sive.rs/obvious). One of Adam's own six named influences per
adampang.com/about.

Also read (2026-08-14, via firecrawl): his `/about` page (sive.rs/about) and
"Spaghetti to Bento," an interview with Alexandre Petit (sive.rs/2025-07-petit),
specifically for how he handles a personal, loaded question ("why have most
of your best friends been women?") rather than an argued position.

**The real pattern, observed across all three:**

- Sentences almost always land solo in their own paragraph. White space is
  structural, not decorative pacing.
- **Bold marks the single sentence that IS the thesis**, as its own
  standalone line. A reader skimming only the bold text gets the whole
  argument. This is the single most useful, most copyable technique here.
- Opens in second person, inside a felt scenario, before stating his own
  view ("Someone asks you to do something, and you feel that pressure to
  answer immediately.").
- One concrete, often odd or vivid image carries the entire abstract point,
  rather than several supporting examples stacked up.
- Never ends on a summary paragraph. Always a direct question to the reader,
  or a short two-line kicker.
- No hedging. Declarative sentences, stated flat.
- Numbered lists appear only when the content is genuinely a sequence or
  checklist, never as decoration.
- Signs off with a short, copy-paste-friendly URL (sive.rs/n0) — pieces are
  built to be quoted atomically, not just read in context.
- The terseness holds even under a personal, potentially loaded question.
  Asked directly why most of his best friends are women, his answer on
  `/about` is one bullet, no defense, no hedge: "Almost all of my best
  friends are women." Asked the same thing in the Petit interview, his
  spoken answer is a single sentence naming a precise feeling and then
  stopping, no follow-up justification. He treats an emotionally loaded
  claim exactly like a technical one: state it accurately, don't pad it.

**What this changes for Pangaea essays going forward:**

1. Add inline **bold** to the one sentence that carries the claim, in essays
   headed for video. That bolded line becomes the on-screen quote / highlight
   beat directly, no separate extraction step needed at script time.
2. Cut paragraphs shorter than the current default. One idea per paragraph,
   sometimes one sentence, more often than not.
3. When an essay leans on abstract argument, look for the one concrete (or
   openly weird) image that could replace three abstract sentences.
4. Keep the existing "no em dashes, title is the claim" rules; they already
   match Sivers' declarative confidence. The gap is paragraph length and the
   bold-thesis marker, not voice.
5. Endings: the "kicker, not a summary" instinct already shows up in several
   Pangaea essays (Early to Big Things, Both Sides Of The Deal) — worth
   naming explicitly as a rule rather than leaving it to instinct each time.
6. For personal or potentially loaded material specifically: state it once,
   accurately, and stop. Don't add a defensive paragraph to a claim that
   doesn't need one just because it's personal; the instinct to over-explain
   grows with how exposed a claim feels, and that's exactly backwards.

## Visakan Veerasamy (visakanv.com) — studied 2026-08

Read: "Being Smart vs Being Kind" (visakanv.com/1000/0675-smart-vs-kind),
"One Must Imagine Sisyphus LOL-ing" (visakanv.com/blog/sisyphus), "How to
Ask for Help" (visakanv.com/blog/help). Note on method: the fetch tool
summarized two of these rather than returning verbatim text, so most of
what follows is pattern-level (confirmed across 3 pieces), with real
verbatim texture only from the opening of the first essay.

**The real pattern:**

- Structurally the opposite of Sivers. Where Sivers is uniformly short and
  clipped, Visakan runs long, meandering, conversational paragraphs, then
  punctuates them with a single short line as a pivot. The real opening of
  "Smart vs Kind": five run-on, self-narrating sentences, then one short
  standalone line: "It took me two decades to really begin to aspire to be
  kind." The contrast in rhythm, not uniform brevity, is the device.
- Argues directly against a specific named source rather than stating his
  own view in isolation. The Sisyphus piece explicitly takes Camus' actual
  line and contests it, proposing an alternative. Sivers rarely names and
  argues against a specific thinker this directly; Visakan builds essays as
  a dialogue with someone else's real claim.
- Titles use irreverent recontextualization of a known reference: "One Must
  Imagine Sisyphus LOL-ing" twists Camus' own famous closing line by
  colliding it with internet vernacular. The wordplay IS the thesis
  preview, not just a hook.
- Specific, numeric self-disclosure embedded mid-argument ("the last 3-5
  years or so"), not vague reflection. Precision about his own life is
  itself part of the persuasion.
- Resolves oppositional pairs into an integration rather than picking a
  side: "smart vs kind" lands on "intelligence gets enriched by kindness,"
  not a verdict for either side. Practical "how to" pieces use unapologetic
  numbered principles, more often and more plainly than Sivers does.

**What this adds, alongside Sivers, for Pangaea essays:**

1. Not every essay needs Sivers' uniform brevity. A long, real, specific
   personal paragraph followed by one short pivot line can hit harder than
   consistent shortness, when the topic is genuinely personal (this matches
   what The Backyard and Pangaea already do instinctively).
2. When an essay is responding to a specific real quote or claim (already
   the case for the Rockefeller mini-film, and for essays like The
   Alexander Phase), consider stating the source's actual claim, then
   arguing with it directly, rather than only synthesizing a position.
3. Titles can recontextualize a known reference (a philosopher's line, a
   cliché, a meme) with a twist, not just state the claim flatly. Different
   from Sivers' clipped two-phrase titles.
4. Precision beats vagueness in self-disclosure. "Two decades" and "3 to 5
   years" land harder than "for a long time" or "recently."

**A different register, same author: "0714 – consider the speedrunners"**
(visakanv.com/1000/0714-consider-the-speedrunners), from his "1,000,000
words" project — a numbered, deliberately unedited, stream-of-consciousness
daily essay, distinct from the polished Substack pieces above.

- Opens with a real personal callback (an old, abandoned blog series about
  games from 2010) before the actual subject starts, not a cold open. The
  essay is allowed to warm up on the page.
- The bulk of the piece is granular, specific observation of someone else
  doing a thing well: watching a Diablo 2 speedrunner, he lists concrete
  mechanical differences (which enemies to farm, that potions stack, which
  boss reliably drops a needed item) rather than a vague "he's just better."
  Specificity is the entire persuasive engine here, same instinct as
  Visakan's "3 to 5 years" over "a long time," just applied to someone
  else's actions instead of his own.
- Narrates the realization live, in real time ("I didn't know this!", "I
  never considered it"), rather than presenting only the finished insight.
  The thinking is visible on the page, not smoothed over afterward.
- The turn: an external, nerdy observation (a stranger's speedrun) becomes a
  direct question about his own life and work ("How do I get better at
  speedrunning my own life?"). The essay's title states its method as
  literally as possible: watch someone who has mastered a system, then ask
  what your own version of that mastery would look like.
- No tidy resolution at the end. He states real indecision plainly ("I don't
  feel strongly about it. But I think I never will, so I have to decide and
  commit.") rather than manufacturing a confident closing line.

**What this adds:** this essay is itself a working example of the exact
loop Adam wants to run generally: watch or read something someone has
mastered, extract the concrete, specific differences (not vague admiration),
and turn it into a question about your own practice. That's the same motion
already used on Sivers, Visakan, and Ferriss in this file, and on Crayon
Capital in `reels/README.md`'s "Picking a topic" section: consume with
enough specificity to name what's actually different, then ask what that
means for what gets made next.

**The variance principle (added 2026-08-19, Adam's own catch).** Bad
writers optimize to minimize negative variance: no bad sentences, nothing
embarrassing, nothing anyone can attack. Good writers optimize to maximize
positive variance: some things land enormously, and the cost of that is
that plenty of others miss. These are different objective functions, and
the first one caps you at inoffensive. Adam named this as his own
recurring mistake.

The antidote, in his words: **"focus on what you want to see more of."**
That matches a real line in *Friendly Ambitious Nerd* almost exactly, from
the "Imagination deficits rule everything around me" section, where
Visakan argues that reading the junk on a timeline and concluding that's
what you should post too is a failure of imagination, and that you should
be posting what you yourself want to see more of.

Note on sourcing: the "what you want to see more of" line is confirmed in
the book. The negative/positive variance framing is Adam's attribution to
Visa and was not found in the pages read for this file, so treat it as
attributed rather than cited until someone finds the original.

**What this changes concretely:** it is a direct argument against the
polish-until-safe instinct, which is exactly what an AI editing pass is
most likely to encourage. Any editor tool built for Pangaea should be
tuned to ask "what is the most alive sentence here, and can more of the
piece be like that" before it asks "what is wrong here." Sanding is how a
piece loses its best line.

## Tim Ferriss (tim.blog) — studied 2026-08

Read: "The Self-Help Trap: What 20+ Years of 'Optimizing' Has Taught Me"
(tim.blog/2026/03/04/the-self-help-trap), "Some Practical Thoughts on
Suicide" (tim.blog/2015/05/06/how-to-commit-suicide). Deliberately skipped
"The Practice of Self-Inquiry," found first, once it turned out to be a
guest post by Dr. Paul Conti, not Tim's own writing.

A fundamentally different length category from Sivers and Visakan: 3,500+
word structured long-form essays, not 150-400 word pieces. The technique
here transfers mostly to the mini-film format, not the short essay.

**The real pattern:**

- Opens with a specific, cinematic, sensory scene, not an abstraction or a
  felt problem stated directly. The Self-Help Trap opens mid-scene: a
  campfire in Montana, a meteor shower starting overhead. The suicide essay
  opens on a specific encounter at a podcast event, with dialogue. Neither
  essay states its thesis before grounding the reader in a real moment.
- Long-form pieces are organized as a genuine multi-act structure with named
  section headers acting as signposts (the suicide essay: "A Twist of
  Fate," "Into the Darkness," "Out of the Darkness"). This is structurally
  the same shape as the Crayon Capital 4-act format already used for
  Pangaea's mini-films, independently confirmed here from a completely
  different source.
- Rhythm shifts deliberately by section: flowing narrative prose for scenes,
  then a hard cut to bulleted or numbered lists when the content is a
  timeline or a set of actionable steps. The list isn't decoration, it's a
  gear change to accelerate pacing at a specific moment.
- Borrows an existing framework and gives it a memorable label rather than
  inventing one from scratch (Maslow's hierarchy, repurposed and re-labeled
  as a "hamburger model"). Matches what the Rockefeller mini-film already
  does by naming his mechanism "the flywheel."
- Endings are graduated, not a single kicker: philosophical reflection, then
  direct address to the reader, then practical resources, then a personal
  sign-off. Appropriate for high-stakes, actionable content where one line
  isn't enough closure. The opposite instinct from Sivers' two-line kicker.

**What this adds, distinct from Sivers and Visakan:**

1. This is direct confirmation, from an independent source, that the mini-film
   4-act structure (cold open -> acts -> outro) is the right shape for
   long-form video, not something invented in isolation.
2. For the mini-film cold opens specifically: open on a real sensory scene
   (already true for Rockefeller and The Backyard), not on the thesis. Keep
   doing this on purpose.
3. For any 300-word essay, the one portable lesson is the scene-first open,
   tested sparingly since it costs word count a short essay can't always
   spare.
4. Name the mechanism. When an essay or film centers on a repeatable system
   (like Rockefeller's flywheel), give it a short, memorable label rather
   than describing it fresh every time it's referenced.

## Summary across all three

Three genuinely different registers, not one "correct" style:
- **Sivers**: short, uniform, bold-marks-the-thesis, ends on a kicker or
  question. Best fit for Pangaea's aphoristic essays.
- **Visakan**: long meandering personal paragraphs punctuated by a short
  pivot line, argues with a named source directly. Best fit for personal
  narrative essays (The Backyard, Pangaea).
- **Ferriss**: long-form, multi-act, scene-first opens, deliberate rhythm
  shifts, graduated endings. Best fit for mini-films, not short essays;
  independently confirms the 4-act mini-film format already in use.

or mini-film, matching the register to what's actually being written.
