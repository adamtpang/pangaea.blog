A video essay demands your eyes: a screen, a chair, a block of undivided time you have to carve out on purpose. A podcast asks for almost nothing. It rides along in the car, on the walk, during the dishes, in whatever dead time already existed in your day. That difference in cost of entry is most of the reason podcasts won the intimacy game.

The other half is voice itself. Hours of someone's unscripted thinking, their pauses, their laugh, their disagreements with a co-host, builds a parasocial closeness that a tightly edited video essay, polished into its best possible thirty seconds per minute, structurally can't. You don't feel like you know a video essayist. You feel like you know a podcast host, because you basically do, the same way you know a friend from years of car rides.

None of this means video essays are worse at their actual job, which is showing you something, not just telling you. It means they're solving a different problem. If the goal is reach into someone's ordinary hours and a feeling of closeness, audio wins by default. If the goal is a specific visual argument, nothing replaces the screen.
