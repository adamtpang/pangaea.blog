If you don't know what it's like to sit on both sides of a deal, it's hard to make deals at all. Most people can explain their own side fluently and the other side not at all. They know what they're getting and can describe it in detail. Ask what the other party gets, and the answer is vague, something like "money" or "exposure," which is almost never the real reason a smart counterparty says yes.

This is the actual work behind something like asap.deals: not finding people who want to transact, that part is easy, but understanding both chairs well enough to structure something both sides would defend to a friend afterward. A deal that only makes sense from one side isn't a deal, it's a pitch waiting to be declined.

Understanding both sides isn't a negotiation trick, it's a comprehension check. If you can't say specifically why the other side is happy to sign, you don't actually understand the deal, you've only understood your half of it. That gap is where deals fall apart later: the side you didn't model turns out to have modeled you completely, and the terms were never as balanced as you assumed.

The best deals are the ones you could argue for from either chair, because you actually know what each side is walking away with. If you can only make your own case, you're not negotiating, you're just hoping the other side doesn't notice you never checked.
